One design system, a branded app for every partner

OmniRetail works with large manufacturers that want a retail app of their own. Guinness, Flour Mills of Nigeria and Lush each have one. I led the retail design team behind them. What made it work was one idea: a new partner is not a new design, it is a new setting on a system we already had. Add: confirm the apps are live on both the Play Store and iOS.

Summary

I led design for the white-label retail apps built on OmniRetail’s design system. When a partner came on, we updated the system’s brand layer, its colours, logo, assets and products, and the components followed. A Figma file built on that gave the partner something real to preview, and the same screens went to the developers and into the store listings. After testing and conversations, partners had preferences, such as letting customers choose pickup during checkout for one merchant, and we added those on top of the shared foundation.

What shipped, and what I can defend

The partner apps are live. What I can defend is the approach and the decisions behind it. One result was reported to me: per-partner delivery time fell by 50%. I do not have the exact before-and-after durations, so it is labelled as reported and I have not added a baseline I cannot show.

The same business, a different brand

Same retail business

Every partner app sells the same way: browse, order, pay, then collect or get delivery. Rebuilding that for each partner would repeat the same work and let the apps drift apart.

Different brand and needs

Each partner has to look like itself, with its own logo, colours and products, and sometimes needs something its customers expect, such as being able to pick an order up.

Constraints

  1. 01 Same Foundation, Every Time Components, patterns and rules stay shared, so one fix reaches every partner app.
  2. 02 Looks Like The Partner Logo, colour and products had to feel like the partner’s own, not OmniRetail with a coat of paint.
  3. 03 Fast To Show A partner needed to see their app early, before anything was built.
  4. 04 Limited At First, Then Room At the start partners could change little. After testing and conversations they usually had preferences, so there had to be a way to say yes.

How it worked

For each new acquisition or vertical, I did not redesign the app. I updated the design system and let the components update to fit. I led the retail design team, worked with another designer, and stayed in close contact with the developers about what had to change.

  1. 01 A Partner Joins A new partner or vertical arrives with its logo, colours, assets and products.
  2. 02 Update The System I update the design system’s brand layer and the components update everywhere to fit.
  3. 03 Preview In Figma A Figma file built on the system shows the partner’s real logo, products and colours, for demos and sign-off.
  4. 04 Hand Off Screens and specs go to the developers, with the images for the store listings.

After testing, partners often had a preference. The One Decision

I chose to change the system, not the screens. A new partner meant updating the brand layer once and letting every component follow, instead of redrawing each app by hand. The trade-off is that partners got little freedom at the start. I accepted that for speed and consistency, and loosened it where testing and conversations showed a real need, as with pickup.

The reported drop says the approach was fast, not why. These are the three measures I would ask for first, in this order.

Time to a signed-off preview

Days from a partner joining to a Figma preview they approve, which is the part this approach speeds up most.

Shared against partner-specific

How much of each app is the shared system and how much is custom, to see whether the foundation is holding.

Requests after testing

How many changes each partner asks for after testing, and which ones repeat across partners.

What would prove this wrong

If partner-specific requests kept growing until each app was close to its own fork, the shared system would not be holding, and fewer, more deliberate options would have been the better call.

Reflection

White-label looks like a theming problem, but the real work is deciding what is allowed to differ. Starting tight and loosening only after real conversations kept the foundation intact and the partners happy. I would write that rule down at the start of the next one.
