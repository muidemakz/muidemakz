Self-Initiated Brief — Weekend Exercise

Two Clicks To Cancel, One Honest Offer To Stay

Most subscription cancellation is built to be hostile—buried three menus deep, a gauntlet of confirmation screens, sometimes a phone call. It works in the short term and costs trust permanently: users who leave easily come back later, users who had to fight their way out tell everyone why they left. This brief redesigns cancellation as a retention argument, not an obstacle course.

The Brief

The Problem

Friction-based retention treats every cancel attempt as a leak to plug. It boosts short-term retention numbers while quietly increasing churn-related complaints, refund disputes, and the number of ex-customers actively warning others off the product.

The Job

Design a cancel flow that's genuinely easy to complete, while still giving the business one fair, well-timed chance to retain the user—then measure whether that trade actually pays off.

Constraints

  1. 01 Two Taps To Cancel Must be findable in two taps from account settings, no support-ticket-only cancellation.
  2. 02 One Offer Only Exactly one retention offer. Not a gauntlet of counter-offers.
  3. 03 Skippable, Useful Reason Ask why they're leaving, but make it skippable, and make the reasons useful to the product team, not just a metric.
  4. 04 Confirm Clearly When access ends, what happens to their data, and how to come back.

The Flow

A conceptual brief, not a built product—these are labeled placeholders standing in for the four screens, not real screenshots.

The One Decision

I chose to cap the retention flow at exactly one offer, matched to the user's stated reason for leaving, instead of a sequence of escalating counter-offers. The common argument against this is that more offers should mean more saves. I'd argue the opposite: a gauntlet signals to the user that the company's default posture is "make it hard to leave," which erodes the goodwill that determines whether they ever come back. One well-matched offer, respected if declined, protects reactivation and word-of-mouth—the two things a multi-step gauntlet actively damages.

What I'd Measure

Completion Rate

% of cancel attempts that complete without hitting a support channel or dead end.

Offer Acceptance

% who accept the single retention offer—tracked per offer type against the stated cancel reason.

Reactivation Rate

% of cancelled users who return within 90 days—the real test of whether easy-exit pays off.

Support Contacts

Volume of "how do I cancel" tickets—should drop toward zero if the entry point is actually visible.

What Would Prove This Wrong

The bet is that easy cancellation increases 90-day reactivation enough to offset whatever short-term retention a harder flow would have squeezed out. If reactivation stayed flat while immediate cancel-through-completion went up, that would mean the old friction was actually converting hesitant-but-recoverable users—and the fix would be a better-timed offer, not a shorter flow.

Reflection

The interesting problem here was never the screens—it's arguing for the user against a short-term business incentive, and doing it with a retention/reactivation business case instead of a moral one. "Dark patterns are bad" doesn't move a product team scored on monthly churn. "Friction-based retention trades reactivation and word-of-mouth for a short-term save" does, because it's the same language the team already uses to judge its own metrics.

Status: Self-initiated design brief, not built for or commissioned by any specific company—modeled on common subscription-cancellation patterns to demonstrate arguing a user-favorable design decision with a business case.
