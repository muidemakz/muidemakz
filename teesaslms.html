Three Rebuilds To Get One LMS Right

Teesas is an EdTech company delivering accessible learning to students across Africa. Schools were coordinating classes, grades, and parent updates across spreadsheets and WhatsApp groups. Teesas needed one Learning Management System that could serve four different user types (students, teachers, parents, administrators) without collapsing under its own feature list. It took three built-and-shipped versions to get there.

As Lead Product Designer, I owned the process end-to-end, research, ideation, high-fidelity delivery, stakeholder buy-in, and usability testing, across all three versions.

My Design Process

A Human-Centered Design process, run three times as the product itself evolved:

  1. 01 Discovery Researched students, teachers, parents, and admins to surface pain points, and studied modern EdTech products for opportunities.
  2. 02 Define Mapped user journeys, built personas, and outlined requirements against a mood board and early wireframes.
  3. 03 Design & Develop Designed role-specific flows for learning, grading, and reporting. Built prototypes, tested with users, iterated.
  4. 04 Deliver Collaborated with the project manager and engineers for smooth handoff, keeping the LMS aligned to both user needs and Teesas's business goals.

Discovery · Research & Insights

User interviews, surveys, and competitor analysis across all four user types surfaced a consistent pattern: everyone's workaround was informal, and none of the workarounds talked to each other.

Problem Statement

How might we design a multi-role LMS that delivers engaging learning experiences, supports teaching efficiency, and provides transparency to parents and administrators, in one system, not four disconnected ones?

Design & Develop · Three Versions

This is the part of the process most case studies skip: the LMS didn't arrive fully formed. It shipped, got used, and told us what was wrong, three times.

The One Decision

Between V2 and V3, I chose to cut features rather than keep expanding them, even though V2's feature list was what had made it feel "market-ready" on paper. The evidence for the cut was V2's own usage data: engagement dropped as flows got more complex, not despite the added functionality but because of it. I argued internally that Teesas's real competitive risk wasn't "too few features" against other EdTech products, it was inconsistent adoption across institutions with very different technical comfort levels. Simplicity was the feature that mattered most for that audience, and it needed to be defended against the instinct to keep adding.

Leading The Process

Scope

Owned the LMS design end-to-end across three shipped versions, not just the screens, but the sequencing: what to prototype fast, what to validate before building, and when to cut rather than add.

Stakeholders

Worked directly with Teesas's CEO and project manager to keep the roadmap grounded in adoption goals, and with engineering to keep each version's scope buildable inside its timeline.

Defending The Call

Simplifying V3 meant arguing to cut functionality stakeholders had already seen and liked in V2, using V2's own engagement drop as the evidence, not a design opinion.

What Followed

This scope of ownership, running iteration cycles, defending tradeoffs with data, aligning cross-functional stakeholders, is what the following year's Design Manager role was built on.

What Would Prove This Wrong

The bet was that simplification, not new functionality, was what would move adoption. If engagement had stayed flat or dropped after V3's cuts, that would have meant clutter wasn't the real blocker, and the next test would have been pricing, onboarding friction, or school-level training instead of interface complexity.

Reflection

This project reinforced that iterative design isn't a slide in a deck, it's shipping something real, watching where it breaks, and being willing to cut what you built the last time. Balancing four user groups with genuinely different needs meant the hardest work wasn't any single screen, it was deciding what NOT to build for V3 after V2 had already proven those features could ship. Great design here was less about adding capability and more about creating flows that let every role succeed without training.
