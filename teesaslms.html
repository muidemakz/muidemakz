Objective, scope & ownership

A student needs to find a lesson. A teacher needs to manage a class. A parent needs to see progress. An administrator needs to run the school. My task was to make these different needs work within one learning platform, from the first version through three further iterations.

Before the LMS, schools coordinated classes, grades and parent updates across spreadsheets and WhatsApp groups. As Lead Product Designer, I was responsible for:

  1. Research and structure. Understand the four user groups, map their journeys, and define how each would navigate the system.
  2. Design and validation. Turn those journeys into role-specific interfaces, prototypes, and usability tests across four versions.
  3. Delivery and iteration. Work with the CEO, project manager, and engineers to align scope, hand off designs, and revise the experience as the product evolved.

The key change came between V2 and V3: I reduced feature clutter and standardized the path from enrollment to assessment. The versions below show how that decision developed; the final screens show how it worked across the different roles.

The starting premise

The team started from the view that schools had no system at all: classes, grades and parent updates lived in spreadsheets and WhatsApp groups, so the job was to replace those workarounds with one platform. Research added a second layer. The workarounds were not only a tooling problem. Each role had a different daily route through the same information, and a platform that ignored that would reproduce the fragmentation inside one product.

Business & user objectives

  1. 01 Create a unified learning experience for students across different age groups.
  2. 02 Empower teachers with tools to manage lessons, assignments, and assessments.
  3. 03 Enable parents to track progress and provide support.
  4. 04 Support administrators with analytics and controls for scaling learning delivery.
  5. 05 Build a system that was intuitive and scalable enough to drive adoption and retention, not just usable in a demo.

Success target: no numeric target was set before release. The team judged each version on adoption across partner schools and on whether each role could complete its core task without support, which is a direction rather than a threshold.

Users & stakeholders

How I investigated and delivered

A Human-Centered Design process, run across four versions as the product itself evolved:

  1. 01 Discovery Researched students, teachers, parents, and admins to surface pain points, and studied modern EdTech products for opportunities.
  2. 02 Define Mapped user journeys, built personas, and outlined requirements against a mood board and early wireframes.
  3. 03 Design & Develop Designed role-specific flows for learning, grading, and reporting. Built prototypes, tested with users, iterated.
  4. 04 Deliver Collaborated with the project manager and engineers for smooth handoff, keeping the LMS aligned to both user needs and Teesas's business goals.

Evidence & research

User interviews, surveys, and competitor analysis across all four user types surfaced a consistent pattern: everyone's workaround was informal, and none of the workarounds talked to each other.

The research pointed to different needs within one connected system: students needed engaging content, teachers needed faster grading and progress tracking, parents lacked visibility, and administrators needed actionable analytics.

Problem Statement

How might we design a multi-role LMS that delivers engaging learning experiences, supports teaching efficiency, and provides transparency to parents and administrators, in one system, not four disconnected ones?

The One Decision

Between V2 and V3, I chose to reduce feature clutter and simplify the core workflows. V2 had added assignments, live sessions, gamification, and reporting, but the experience had become harder to navigate. For V3, I simplified navigation and standardized the journey from enrollment through assessment.

The trade-off was breadth versus clarity: students, teachers, parents, and administrators needed different tools, but each still needed a clear route to their everyday tasks. Simplifying those routes was the design response to V2's complexity. The outcome section lists reported product measures, but they do not prove that interface changes alone caused every change in engagement, and the figures are reported for V4 as a whole rather than attributed to individual screens.

Leadership, trade offs & delivery

Scope

Owned the LMS design end-to-end across four shipped versions. That covered more than the screens. It included the sequencing: what to prototype fast, what to validate before building, and when to cut rather than add.

Stakeholders

Worked directly with Teesas's CEO and project manager to keep the roadmap grounded in adoption goals, and with engineering to keep each version's scope buildable inside its timeline.

Defending The Call

Simplifying V3 meant arguing to cut functionality stakeholders had already seen and liked in V2. The argument rested on V2's own engagement drop, read from product usage reporting rather than from a design opinion. The measure, period and source behind that drop were not documented, so I present it as the reason for the call, not as a proven causal link.

What It Built

Running iteration cycles, defending trade-offs with data and aligning cross-functional stakeholders is the work this project demanded. That is the part of the job I would point to when asked what design leadership looked like here, rather than any single screen.

What Would Prove This Wrong

The bet was that simplification, not new functionality, was what would move adoption. If engagement had stayed flat or dropped after V3's cuts, that would have meant clutter wasn't the real blocker, and the next test would have been pricing, onboarding friction, or school-level training instead of interface complexity.

Reflection

This project reinforced that iterative design isn't a slide in a deck. It's shipping something real, watching where it breaks, and being willing to cut what you built the last time. Balancing four user groups with genuinely different needs meant the hardest work wasn't any single screen. It was deciding what NOT to build for V3 after V2 had already proven those features could ship. The design aim was less about adding capability and more about making each role's core flow clear, without assuming that more features would solve the gaps.
