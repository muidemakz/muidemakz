Independent Concept · Conversational Payments

Small design touches that make a chat payment easier to check

Nsimbi, a mobile money app by Felix Kitaka, lets you ask for airtime, voice and data in one chat message, then review and approve each purchase. The idea already worked. I looked at what stands between a person and a confident tap on Buy, and added design touches that make each payment easier to read, check and correct.

This is an independent concept inspired by Felix’s public demo. It is not commissioned, built or endorsed by him. Accounts, balances, PINs and payments are all simulated, and the tests used simulated users, so every result here is predicted.

Summary

Nsimbi already gets the hard part right: three purchases from one message, each approved on its own. This project is about the space around that. Where does a person look before they approve? What do they need to see to be sure? What happens when something goes wrong? Small answers to those questions are what make a payment feel safe to tap.

I watched Felix’s recorded demo closely, kept what worked, and made four design touches. Then I built the result in code so the interactions were real, rebuilt it in Figma as a documented system, and checked it with simulated users. This is a design hypothesis, not a proven customer problem: I had a product recording and public posts, and did no interviews.

My Design Flow

I work in four moves: observe, build, document, check. Each pass has its own tool and answers a different question, and I started from what the product already does.

Pass 1 · 30 October

Bearings

What is the product doing, and what should I keep?

I screen-recorded Felix’s demo and watched it frame by frame, sampling every ten seconds and looking closer at the key moments. I wrote down what was visible and, as importantly, what was not, before designing anything.

I chose one journey, three purchases from one message, because it is the clearest version of what makes Nsimbi different. For the chat-and-payment structure I read a reference pattern in Figma, and I picked four reference screens for hierarchy and restraint. I kept their structure and left their branding, artwork and content out.

Eight questions, written first. People should be able to answer these from the screen alone:

  1. What purchases did the app understand from the request?
  2. Which purchase needs approval now?
  3. How much leaves the wallet for it?
  4. When and on which network can the voice minutes be used?
  5. Which purchases are done and which remain?
  6. Where can I find a receipt?
  7. If I skip one, can I tell skipped from paid without colour?
  8. If a status is uncertain, does the screen avoid claiming failure or inviting a second payment?

Pass 2 · 30 October to 2 November

Code

Does the idea still work when it moves?

I built the prototype in code, not in Figma, because the idea depends on things a still frame cannot show: typing a request, a queue that advances, a sheet that opens for each purchase, PIN entry, receipts, skipping, an uncertain state, and swiping between chat and wallet.

It started as a small system: icons, buttons and inputs, then purchase rows, review details and receipts, then the header, balance, composer and sheets. Colour, type, spacing and radius live as tokens, with light as the default and a dark switch. The same Review and Receipt serve every purchase type.

At the first delivery I measured 16 contrast pairs across both themes and checked layout at 13 widths from 320 to 2560 pixels. On 1 November 76 automated tests passed. Those checks show the demo behaves as built. They say nothing about whether people understand it.

From 31 October I iterated daily: statements and sharing, profile and verification tiers, wallet and Send money, a daily reward, a recent banks shortcut, and validation messages shown beside the button they block.

Pass 3 · 2 November

Back to Figma

Can I write the system down, and does it hold up?

With the design settled in code, I rebuilt it in Figma as the reference file, in three pages. Foundations holds colour in light and dark, effects and type. Components runs from atoms to templates. Screens covers nine flows, each in light and dark: sign in, dashboard, daily reward, conversation, purchase, statement and the rest.

What the round trip showed. Writing a system down is how you find where it drifted:

  • The type page lists about 70 named styles, including 15 near-identical Display sizes. A healthy scale is closer to ten. The code grew a new size each time a screen needed one.
  • Component variants are named after their padding values instead of their purpose, so the list is long and hard to scan.

So the Figma file documents the system as built, and it marks the first clean-up to do before this goes to a team: collapse the type scale and name variants by what they are for.

Four Design Touches, Original And Update

Each touch is a small change to something the original already did. The original is on the left, my update on the right, and under them is what the change rests on.

Touch 1 of 4

Keep the whole request in view

One message asked for three things, so all three should stay visible while you decide on the first.

In the original

The other two purchases sit inside a plan that is collapsed behind View 3 payments, out of sight until you open it.

Three purchases, one open

The open purchase shows its fields. The other two wait below as short rows with their position, for example 2 of 3, and a Do this instead button. The reply says all three can be edited and approved on their own.

Where this comes from

Original: the plan is collapsed behind View 3 payments. Update: the summary line and the waiting rows are my additions.

Not yet tested

Whether people read the waiting rows as still unpaid, not as done.

Touch 2 of 4

Put every money detail at the decision

The decision to pay should happen next to everything the payment touches.

In the original

The card shows amount, fees and total debit, then Buy and Skip. It also says the quote is valid for 5 minutes.

One sheet per purchase

Who is paid, on which network, where the money comes from, the amount, the fee, the total debit and the balance afterwards, with Edit details beside Continue. The sheet says approval and payment are simulated, and one approval never covers all three.

Where this comes from

The figures are the recorded demo’s: UGX 100, a UGX 0 fee, and a balance of UGX 73,910.68 falling to UGX 73,810.68. The extra rows and the edit action are mine.

Still missing

The original’s 5 minute quote validity. My sheet does not show it yet.

Touch 3 of 4

Make the restriction impossible to miss

A restriction that changes whether a purchase is a good buy belongs where the decision is made.

In the original

The 30 minute voice bundle only works from 11 PM to 7 AM, MTN to MTN. The original says so in a sentence above the card and in two small lines under the number.

A notice before Review

A quiet notice with a clock icon sits under the amount, right above Review purchase. Skip stays beside it, so declining one item does not end the queue.

Where this comes from

The terms are the recording’s. Turning them into a distinct notice before review is my change.

Not yet tested

Whether people notice the restriction more than the original’s small print.

Touch 4 of 4

Say when a payment is uncertain, and never offer to pay twice

If a confirmation is interrupted, the screen should say what is known and offer a check, not a second payment.

In the original

Nothing. The recording does not show an interrupted or uncertain payment.

Check, never pay twice

The card says the confirmation was interrupted and offers Check demo status and View receipt. It does not claim failure and shows no second Pay button. Paid, skipped, waiting and uncertain all use words as well as colour.

Where this comes from

An addition. It is not in Felix’s recording and does not describe how Nsimbi works.

Not yet tested

The simulated test runs did not cover this state.

The Journey, Start To Finish

One fictional user, one request and the same amounts and providers throughout. Airtime is UGX 100, the voice bundle is 30 minutes for UGX 300, and the data bundle is 30 MB for UGX 250, all taken from the recording.

Then the queue moves to the next purchase and the same loop repeats. The uncertain, failed and statement states are extensions I added around this loop, not behaviour from Felix’s recording.

Go through Nsimbi yourself

Open the prototype on your phone, or in a narrow window. Enter any username, then any four digits. On the dashboard, tap “Try the three-purchase demo” and approve each purchase on its own. Everything is fictional, and the PIN and payments are simulated.

What This Shows I Can Do

The touches are small on purpose. The work is in finding them, building them so they can be felt, and being honest about how far the evidence goes.

01

Read a product closely

Frame-by-frame observation, a written list of what is and is not visible, and eight comprehension questions set before any design.

02

Design the interaction

Queues, review sheets, restrictions at the point of decision and honest uncertain states, each tied to a question people should be able to answer.

03

Build a design system

Tokens, atoms to templates, light and dark, and the same Review and Receipt reused for every purchase type.

04

Prototype in code

Real typing, sheets, PIN entry, receipts, skipping and swiping, so the interactions can be felt and tested, not only imagined.

05

Document it in Figma

Foundations, Components and Screens rebuilt from the code, with the drift the round trip exposed written down.

06

Check it and say what it shows

Seven simulated test runs, findings read over scores, and a clear list of what the evidence cannot show.

One Flow I Calibrated: The Purchase PIN

The clearest example is the PIN that confirms each purchase. The first run scored 75. I changed what the run covers, and the second scored 99. Here is what that gap does and does not say about the design.

Run 1 · PIN asked

75

All five stopped at the PIN that confirms a purchase.

Run 2 · No PIN

99

All five finished the whole journey.

The gap

+24

All of it from one step. No screen changed.

The first score

Every participant stopped at the PIN, so the flow scored 75. A simulated participant will not enter a PIN, so that number measured the PIN prompt, not the journey.

Make the score mean something

I changed what the run covers, not what a person sees. In a test mode the prototype approves a purchase without asking for the PIN, so the run covers the whole journey.

What changed

The score went from 75 to 99. Nothing on screen changed between the two runs.

The gap is the PIN

The design did not get better. The question changed from can they get past the PIN to can they finish the journey.

What it cannot say

A 99 with the PIN removed says nothing about whether people trust the payment step.

Keep the PIN, test it with people

The step stays in the design. Real participants are the only way to test it, so I report both scores and read the gap as the PIN.

Method in detail

One study, “Nsimbi Chat-First Purchases”, run on 1 November 2026 against the public prototype. Each run was five simulated sessions of the same persona. I changed the task text between runs, so each run is its own test and I do not average them. The figures come from the Valideity reports, which sit in a private workspace.

  • Test switches. The public prototype has two switches that do nothing for a normal visitor: one skips the simulated PIN, and one plants the UGX 1,500 fee. They exist so these runs can be repeated.
  • Send money was left out. The test cannot enter a bank account number, so that flow was not covered.

How I Designed It

I built it with my own design skill

UI Design with MM is a portable design skill I wrote. It works with Claude, Codex and other compatible assistants, and takes a design from questions and research, through design tokens, to Figma or code. It is MIT licensed.

It was the method for the code pass. Here is what that looked like:

  • Settle the direction before the screens. The question of which journey to build was answered before any polished screen existed.
  • Use references, do not copy them. Each reference was logged with what I took from it and what I left out: the branding, artwork and content.
  • Record what I could not establish. The original font and screen scale were unknowable from a recording, so I noted them and claimed no pixel match.
  • Check for generic output. Every repeated pattern had to earn its place. A decorative chart or an invented illustration did not.

Next Steps

The design is not finished. These are the areas I would take on next:

Fix Open Findings

Show a real amount instead of a grey placeholder, explain the empty contacts tab, and give the disabled button a plain reason.

Keep What Worked

The original tells you a quote is valid for 5 minutes. My review sheet does not yet.

Matched Comparison

Rebuild Felix’s flow as an interactive copy and test both with the same request, facts and questions.

Real People

Test the payment step with real participants and no demo switch, because the PIN is where trust is decided.

Figma System

Collapse the type scale to about ten styles and name variants by purpose.

The tension I kept coming back to is not conversation against control. It is giving people enough visibility and correction at the moment of a money decision, without making them manage every step. Small, specific touches are how a design gets there.
