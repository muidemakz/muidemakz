Making account verification status and limits clearer

Partway through a Level 3 account upgrade, one screen tells a merchant they have reached Level 3 and that they are still on Level 2. I redesigned the status, limits and next-step moments of that journey, then tested the result against the original.

This is an independent exploration, rebuilt from supplied product captures. It is not commissioned or official Moniepoint work, and the tests used synthetic sessions, so every result is predicted usability evidence.

Summary

Moniepoint accounts come in levels, and a higher level unlocks higher limits. A merchant midway through an upgrade asks a simple question: where do I stand? The original journey gave three different answers. A title welcomed them to Level 3, a notice said they were on Level 2, and the limits card showed only the limits they did not have yet.

I rebuilt the journey in Figma from supplied captures, changed six things the evidence and the supplied facts supported, and ran the original and the alternative through matched synthetic tests. The personal fields and media are fictional, and the camera, location and processing steps are simulated. No real banking, identity verification or location service was performed.

Why This Flow

Public Android reviews of Moniepoint’s business app describe two kinds of trouble: face verification that fails, and address or document verification that is hard to complete (read the reviews). Reviews show a symptom, not a cause. They do not establish what causes the problem, how common it is or whether it persists, so I treated them as a reason to look and not as a diagnosis.

This redesign targets how well people understand their status, limits and next step. It does not claim to fix face verification failures, which need engineering and product work.

Original Customer Feedback

These public Google Play reviews prompted the investigation. Two distinct reviewer profiles reported face-verification failures in October 2026. A separate September review described repeated utility-bill rejection during an account upgrade. They report different symptoms, not one established cause.

From feedback to design question. These Android reports motivated investigation of verification. The status-wording issue came from inspecting the supplied iOS journey. The synthetic findings below assess the reconstruction; they do not prove a cause or resolution of these reviewers' failures.

I rebuilt the Level 3 upgrade end to end, from the first screen to the dashboard. Then I ran a first test: five synthetic sessions of one persona, a small-business owner in Lagos on an entry-level Android phone with an unstable network.

What The First Test Found

All five sessions finished the task, so completion told me little. The report listed 17 friction findings, which I triaged and did not treat as 17 defects. Some came from limits of the reconstruction, such as buttons that did nothing in a click-through prototype. I picked three I could address with the facts I had.

Partner Consent

OkHi appears mid flow and asks for background location with little explanation. The report flagged it, and all five sessions reached that screen.

Late Legal Text

A dense indemnity page follows a screen that says verification has started. Several sessions described accepting without finishing reading.

Mixed Status Wording

The dashboard says Level 2. A later screen says Welcome To Level 3 and also says you are currently on Level 2. Only 3 of 5 sessions reached that screen, so I also checked it directly in the reconstruction.

I led with status because the contradiction is visible in the reconstruction and could be tested with a small, comparable change. Consent mattered too, but better answers needed facts I did not have.

Six Decisions, Before And After

I reused the existing components and kept every figure on screen exactly as supplied. The one new component is a limits card. Drag any comparison, press its Before or After tag, or pick a change to see where it lives on the screen.

Decision 1 of 6

Say what is true right now

The status screen made two claims at once. The title welcomed the merchant to Level 3 while the notice underneath said they were still on Level 2. I rewrote both to agree, and to say what happens next.

What changed

Evidence · what the simulated testers said afterwards

In the Before run, 13 of 20 answers pointed out the contradiction between the Welcome or Level 3 badge and Level 2. In the After run, none of the 20 did, including none of the 18 who saw the new status screen.

Decision 2 of 6

Show what is available now beside what comes later

The original showed only the Level 3 limits, so a merchant on Level 2 saw amounts they could not use yet. The new limits card separates the two states and keeps the supplied figures. I invented no policy.

What changed

Evidence · what the simulated testers said afterwards

9 of 20 Before answers gave the Level 3 amounts. The other 11 did not, and most said they never saw them. In the After run 19 of 20 gave them, including all 18 who reached the new screen.

Decision 3 of 6

Tell people what to do, and make the banner work

While the address check runs, the dashboard banner reported progress but gave no instruction, and in the Before prototype tapping it did nothing. I added one line and made the whole banner open the status screen.

What changed

Evidence · with a caveat

18 of 20 After answers described using the banner to reach status. In the Before run, 16 of 20 described tapping a dashboard control and getting no response, and 2 found a way through the level chip. Part of that difference is a repaired prototype link, not better wording, and the new line says “tap”, so some answers may echo it.

Decision 4 of 6

Separate the finished check from the one still running

This screen celebrated verified proof of address and, in the same breath, talked about provisional limits. The new wording says which check is done, which is still in progress, and what level the merchant is on for now.

What changed

Evidence · not scored on its own

I did not score this change separately. It is there to keep the wording consistent, and I do not count it as a result.

Decision 5 of 6

Warn about the legal step before it arrives

After a screen saying verification had started, a dense legal page appeared. I left the legal text alone. I cannot verify what it should say, and I did not want to invent an exit that may not exist. The only changes are a signpost and a button label.

What changed

Evidence · partial

11 of the 18 After participants who reached the page recalled being told another step was coming, against none in the Before run. 7 of 18 still found the page unexpected. The legal wording, including “the Request”, is unchanged, and the report still flags the indemnity term as unexplained for 13 of 20 in both versions.

Decision 6 of 6

Name the partner

OkHi appears mid flow and asks about background location. I said what it is in the introduction and kept everything else as supplied. I added no privacy, retention, optional-access or eligibility assurances, because I have no facts to back them.

What changed

Evidence · no change seen

All 20 Before answers could already say what OkHi does from the screen text, and so could all 18 After participants who reached it. How long OkHi keeps location data, and whether location access is mandatory, remain open and need product answers.

Deliberately Left Alone

Whether location access is mandatory or optional, how long OkHi keeps location data, what the indemnity text should say, the dead end behind View More on the Level 3 entry screen, the findings about the camera shutter, and permission rows that look tappable. Each needs a product, legal or engineering answer, or reflects a limit of the reconstruction. Design copy cannot settle them.

What The Test Showed

I tested the original journey the same way as the redesign: the same task, the same device and the same five follow-up questions. Each version had 20 simulated sessions across six merchant personas, run in a live browser. I read every raw answer, not only the score.

A smaller pilot came first: five sessions per version, one persona, scoring 80 and 82. It pointed the same way on the limits, the contradiction and finding status. I report the larger run as the study and do not add the two together, because the personas and the simulator version were different.

What Weakens The Result

  • Not everyone saw the new screens. Two After sessions stopped on the Level 3 entry screen, which I did not change, after tapping View More. So 18 of 20 saw the redesigned screens.
  • Simulated participants. Six personas, 3 or 4 sessions each. Differences between personas are leads, not findings. Five of the six personas were written by me, so the result depends on how I described them.
  • Targeted questions. The five follow-ups were asked after the task and were about the topics I changed, so the answers partly reflect what I asked.
  • One person sorting the answers, not blind. I sorted the answers myself, with a keyword search and a manual read, knowing which version each came from.
  • Stand-in photos and a repaired link. The camera and document steps use fixed stand-in media, and part of the status gain comes from a repaired prototype link.
  • I could not watch the After play back. The Figma player would not render in my test browsers, so I checked how the screens connect in the Figma file instead.
  • No business outcome. I measured no conversion, verification approval, revenue or support-volume change.

  • The score does not measure what I changed. I cannot say how big a gap has to be to matter at 20 sessions. Fewer issues in the After partly come from the repaired dashboard link, which removes controls that did nothing in the Before prototype.
  • Two rounds, reported separately. Valideity would not let me add sessions to the pilot runs, because the study sections or target had changed since, and I do not know what changed. So the larger study is a fresh run. The simulator was also updated between the two rounds, so differences between them are not design effects.
  • Two days. The two versions ran on 27 and 28 October. Both used Valideity’s standard setting for how much simulated people differ from each other.
  • Earlier scores are not controls. A first full-journey run scored 89, a focused camera pilot scored 80, and a camera wording comparison scored 86 in both versions with no improvement. Their tasks, questions and fixtures differed, so I do not compare against them.
  • Research questions. Valideity’s automatic AI check of my questions was not read in detail, and I do not use it as evidence.

How I Designed It

I built it with my own design skill

UI Design with MM is a portable design skill I wrote. It works with Claude, Codex and other compatible assistants, and takes a design from questions and research, through design tokens, to Figma or code. It is MIT licensed.

I used it as the method for rebuilding the original journey. Here is what that looked like in practice:

  • Measure before editing. The source screens were measured, and nothing in the source was added, removed or renamed without approval.
  • Keep personal media out. Raw face, account and address captures stayed local. The prototype uses labelled stand-ins and simulated camera, location and processing.
  • Record what I could not establish. The original font family and production timing were unknowable from captures, so I noted them instead of presenting approximations as matches.
  • Hand off against evidence. The skill ends with a checklist, and nothing counts as done until each item is ticked with proof.

What I Would Do Next

  • Test with real merchants. Put the redesigned status and limits screens in front of them. Synthetic sessions show where to look, not what people will do.
  • Fix the dead end behind View More. On the Level 3 entry screen, four After participants tapped it and reached the prototype note, and two of them gave up there.
  • Get product answers first. Mandatory versus optional location, and how long location data is kept, before I write more copy about either.
  • Ask a legal owner for a plain-language summary of the indemnity text. It is not mine to write.

I started from evidence rather than taste, changed only what the facts allowed, tested it against the original with the same questions, and read the raw answers when the headline score barely moved. I also wrote down what did not move.
