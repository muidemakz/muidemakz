Bringing Salary Advance into Fundall's mobile app

“I can only take a loan on the web app. It's frustrating to always go to my browser whenever I want to apply for Salary Advance.” That was real QA feedback on Fundall, an all-in-one financial partner for individuals, SMEs, and corporates, and it's the sentence that shaped this project.

Summary

Salary Advance already existed on Fundall's web app. Retail customers kept leaving the mobile app to find it, and some told us so directly. I designed the mobile borrowing journey that brought it into the app: a visible entry point, eligibility shown before the application, and advance history in one place. It shipped on iOS, and the company reported 28% more advance requests in the first quarter after launch.

Problem

Fundall was mid-rebrand after an acquisition, and the app was being rebuilt around the new brand. Borrowing was the awkward part: the product existed, but on mobile it was neither visible nor self-explanatory. Customers were opening a browser to do something the app could have done for them.

Primary task

Design and integrate Loans & Salary Advance into the existing Fundall app so it is visible and frictionless to use.

Secondary task

Make the mobile app reflect the new brand direction without losing the familiarity of the flows customers already knew.

How we knew it was real. Three sources pointed the same way: direct interviews with Fundall customers, internal QA feedback shared in the team's testing channel, and the product brief for the rebrand. The clearest signal was a quote from a QA session that is reproduced below. It described a behaviour, not a preference: people were leaving the app to complete a task they wanted to complete in the app.

What the evidence did not tell us. It told us access was a problem. It did not tell us how many people were affected, or whether a visible entry point alone would change behaviour. I treated those as open questions and went looking for a design that could be tested rather than a redesign that assumed the answer.

My role & constraints

I was the designer on the feature, from the first research conversation to the build handoff. Fundall had a small product team, so the work was hands-on rather than art-directed: I ran the research synthesis, drew the flows and wireframes, built the high-fidelity screens, tested them on iOS, and sat with engineering through implementation.

Scope

Loans and Salary Advance inside the mobile app, including the home entry point, eligibility, application and advance history.

Team

Fundall's product, engineering and QA teams, working alongside the acquiring company's design team on the rebrand.

Constraint

iOS first, on a rebrand timeline the team did not fully control. Android was not designed in this scope, and I flagged that early rather than after handoff.

Shared system

I contributed to the merged design system: auditing overlapping components and aligning tokens with the engineers building them.

The interesting constraint was not the screen count. It was that two companies' patterns had to become one while a customer-facing feature was being built on top of them. That pushed the work toward reusing what already existed instead of introducing a third pattern nobody owned.

Key decisions

Three decisions shaped the feature. Each one cost something, and each is visible in the screens that follow.

01 · Give borrowing its own destination

Borrowing became a visible entry on the home dashboard with its own page, instead of a link buried in a menu.

Trade-off: the dashboard had to carry a promotional block on every visit, including for customers who would never borrow. I argued for it with the QA feedback rather than a design preference.

02 · Show the eligible amount before applying

Eligibility and the available amount appear before the application form, so the decision starts with a number the customer can act on.

Trade-off: a number shown early can anchor someone on an amount that may change after checks. I kept the eligibility step separate from approval and made the final amount explicit at confirmation.

03 · Design for iOS first

The scope was iOS, which let the team ship inside the rebrand window instead of waiting for a cross-platform pass.

Trade-off: some interactions would not scale to Android as drawn. That was a known debt, not a discovery after launch.

The decision I would revisit

Putting borrowing on the dashboard assumed the blocker was visibility. The interviews supported that, but visibility and trust are different problems. If adoption had not moved, the next fix would have been clarity about fees and repayment, not more placement. Keeping those two bets separate in the outcome section is deliberate: the numbers show what happened, not which of the two caused it.

Design

The journey follows the order a customer meets it: sign in, home, the borrowing page, the eligibility and onboarding steps, the application, then confirmation with the advance visible in history. Before and after screens are paired where a change is the point.

Structure & system

The flow was drawn before the screens, so the team could agree on what a customer would see at each step. The design system work ran alongside it: two companies' components had to become one set, so the feature was built on shared foundations instead of a local variant.

What testing changed. Two findings mattered. First, users liked the speed of the new request flow, which supported the visibility bet. Second, they could not explain how their limit had been set, and that is a trust problem no amount of placement fixes. I recorded it as the next thing to design rather than pretending the flow was finished.

What this does not show

It does not isolate the cause. The feature launched during a rebrand, alongside a new app build and a marketing push, so some of the lift may belong to any of those. It does not cover Android, because Android was not designed here. And it does not show whether visibility or trust did the work. A follow-up study would separate the two rather than credit the redesign for all of it.

What would prove this wrong

The bet was that visibility, not appetite, was the blocker: users already wanted the feature and just could not find it. If adoption had not moved after making it a primary dashboard entry point, the QA complaints would have been about web-only friction rather than discoverability, and the next fix would have been trust or eligibility clarity instead of placement.

Reflection

The part I am proudest of is not a screen. It is that the feature shipped inside a rebrand without adding a third set of patterns to the product. The part I would do differently is the measurement. I reported the lift after launch, but I did not agree a baseline and a comparison group with the product team before the work started, which is why the outcome section has to lean on company reporting rather than a number I can defend line by line.

The other lesson is about scope. Designing iOS first was the right call for the timeline, and it left a known Android debt. Saying that in the room, before the build, was more useful than discovering it in QA.

Next tests

Explain the limit

Show how an eligible amount was calculated, since not understanding the limit was the clearest trust gap in testing.

Baseline before launch

Agree the baseline, comparison window and success measure with product before the next release, so the result can be attributed rather than reported.

Design Android deliberately

Rebuild the interactions that do not translate, instead of shrinking the iOS pattern.
